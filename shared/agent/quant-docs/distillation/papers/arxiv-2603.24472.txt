                                                    Self-distillation has emerged as an effective post-training paradigm for
                                                    LLMs, often improving performance while shortening reasoning traces.
                                                    However, in mathematical reasoning, we find that it can reduce response
                                                    length while degrading performance. We trace this degradation to the sup-
                                                    pression of epistemic verbalization—the model’s expression of uncertainty
                                                    during reasoning. Through controlled experiments varying condition-
                                                    ing context richness and task coverage, we show that conditioning the
                                                    teacher on rich information suppresses uncertainty expression, enabling
                                                    rapid in-domain optimization with limited task coverage but harming OOD
                                                    performance, where unseen problems benefit from expressing uncertainty
                                                    and adjusting accordingly. Across Qwen3-1.7B/8B, DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-
                                                    7B, and Olmo3-7B-Instruct, we observe performance drops of up to 40%.
                                                    Our findings highlight that exposing appropriate levels of uncertainty is
                                                    crucial for robust reasoning and underscore the importance of optimizing
                                                    reasoning behavior beyond merely reinforcing correct answer traces.


                                         1       Introduction
                                         Recently, self-distillation (Snell et al., 2022) has attracted increasing attention in the post-
                                         training of large language models (LLMs). In this paradigm, two instances of the same model
                                         are employed: one conditioned on privileged information (e.g., ground-truth solutions)
                                         serves as a teacher, providing dense reward signals for responses generated by another
                                         instance that does not have access to that information. Several studies have demonstrated
                                         that combining this framework with post-training methods such as Reinforcement Learning
                                         from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) leads to highly efficient performance gains (Zhu et al., 2026;
                                         Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Penaloza et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026).
                                         These methods have shown particularly strong improvements in domains such as agentic
                                         environments and scientific reasoning, especially under in-domain evaluation settings.

Interestingly, a consistent trend observed across these works is that performance improves
as response length decreases, suggesting that self-distillation promotes more concise and
effective reasoning.
However, when we apply the same self-distillation approach to mathematical reasoning
tasks, we observe a markedly different phenomenon. Figure 1 compares the effects of a
representative self-distillation algorithm, SDPO, in the Chemistry domain (a) and the Math
domain (b). As shown in the figure, in the Chemistry domain, self-distillation substantially
reduces response length compared to GRPO while rapidly improving performance. In
contrast, in the Math domain, although response length consistently decreases as training
progresses, performance drops significantly, contrary to prior findings.
This raises a question: ”Why does performance sometimes degrade despite the model being
trained to move toward the correct answer?”
Our analysis reveals a consistent pattern: the more informative the context provided to the
teacher, the more concise and confident the resulting reasoning becomes, with substantially
fewer expressions of uncertainty and, particularly in math reasoning, degraded performance.
We trace this effect to the suppression of epistemic verbalization (Kim et al., 2026), whereby
models explicitly verbalize and incorporate uncertainty during reasoning. Strong reasoning
models such as DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) frequently express uncertainty using tokens
like “Wait” or “Hmm”. Although these expressions may not directly advance the reasoning,
removing them discards important signals that a reasoning path may be flawed, leading to
significant performance drops (Kim et al., 2026).
To systematically understand when and why self-distillation suppresses epistemic verbal-
ization and thus affects performance, we conduct a comprehensive empirical study and
identify two key factors: information richness and task coverage. The more privileged
the information the teacher receives, such as the correct solution, the more its reasoning
traces suppress expressions of uncertainty, encouraging the student to imitate a confident
reasoning style that presupposes information unavailable at inference time. When task
coverage is limited, this compression enables rapid in-domain optimization. However, as
coverage increases, removing epistemic verbalization can interfere with optimization across
diverse tasks, degrading performance on more challenging or unseen problems.
More broadly, our results show that even when the training objective faithfully guides the
model toward correct reasoning traces, the resulting reasoning style can quietly shift in
ways that hurt generalization. The suppression of epistemic verbalization is not penalized
by standard objectives, yet negatively impacts out-of-distribution (OOD) performance. This
suggests that post-training objectives need to account not only for answer correctness, but
also for eliciting and preserving uncertainty-aware reasoning behaviors. We believe these
findings offer a useful step toward a deeper understanding of reasoning in self-distillation
and post-training more broadly.

Key Characteristics of Math Reasoning               In LLMs, math reasoning can be viewed as
self-Bayesian reasoning, where each step is generated conditioned only on the prompt x and
previously generated tokens y = (y1 , . . . , y T ), allowing the model to iteratively update its
belief over intermediate hypotheses (Kim et al., 2026). Such reasoning spans diverse tasks
such as arithmetic, algebra, geometry, word problems, and logical pattern recognition, so
evaluation benchmarks are frequently OOD relative to training data due to compositional
and reasoning-depth shifts.
Within this process, verbalized uncertainty toward correct response y∗ , referred to as epis-
temic verbalization (Kim et al., 2026), can serve as an informative signal rather than stylistic
redundancy. As shown in Figure 2(2a), reasoning without such signals may prematurely
commit to incorrect hypotheses with little room for recovery, whereas epistemic verbaliza-
tion preserves alternative hypotheses and supports gradual uncertainty reduction.

3   LLM Reasoning Behavior Under Richer Information
In self-distillation, the teacher has access to a richer context c, which provides strong hints
and enables it to generate reasoning traces with minimal expressed uncertainty (Figure
2(2b)). While this yields more concise responses, it could eliminate not only unnecessary
verbosity but also valuable epistemic signals, potentially hindering the student’s ability to
perform uncertainty-aware reasoning. This raises two questions we investigate: (i) how
does the richness of c relate to the suppression of uncertainty expression and to reasoning
performance? and (ii) if epistemic verbalization is beneficial, why does suppressing it
improve performance in domains such as Chemistry while degrading it in Math?
To study these questions, we first need a way to quantify how informative the conditioning
context is. Let y∗ denote a random variable representing an ideal correct response to x. We
formalize the information that c provides about y∗ as the conditional mutual information
                          I (y∗ ; c | x ) = H (y∗ | x ) − H (y∗ | x, c),                 (2)
which captures the reduction in uncertainty about y∗ once the additional context c is given.
Building on this measure, we construct a controlled comparison to examine how the model’s
reasoning behavior changes as the conditioning information is varied. We use the DAPO-
Math-17k dataset (Yu et al., 2026) with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (Guo et al., 2025) as
the base model, selecting 100 problems on which the base model achieves accuracy between
0.125 and 0.5 over 8 rollouts. Let s denote the full solution (including chain-of-thought
in <think> tags), s\think the solution with <think> content removed, and yr ∼ πθ (· | x, s)
a response previously generated under full solution guidance. We compare the model’s
responses across four generation settings:
• (1) Unguided generation (c = ∅): I (y∗ ; c | x ) = 0 by definition.
• (2) Solution-guided generation (c = s): provides maximal guidance and yields the largest
  I ( y ∗ ; c | x ).
• (3) Solution-guided generation without think contents (c = s\think ): since s\think is a
  strict informational subset of s, we have I (y∗ ; s\think | x ) ≤ I (y∗ ; s | x ).
• (4) Regeneration-conditioned generation (c = yr , where yr ∼ πθ (· | x, s)): since yr is
  derived from s, the data processing inequality gives I (y∗ ; yr | x ) ≤ I (y∗ ; s | x ).
These settings induce the following ordering over the conditional mutual information:

Epistemic Tokens       Following Kim et al. (2026), we define a set of 10 epistemic markers
T = {wait, hmm, perhaps, maybe, actually, alternatively, seems, might, likely, check} as practi-
cal indicators of regions where the externalization of uncertainty is likely to occur. These
tokens are intuitively recognizable as uncertainty expressions, with their selection further
supported by a cross-family analysis of 9,600 reasoning traces (Appendix B). We measure
the epistemic token count of a response y as E(y) = ∑t∈T count(t, y).

    Takeaway 1: Information Richness and Epistemic Verbalization
    As the conditioning context c becomes more informative and directly useful, the LLM generates
    answers more confidently and with fewer expressions of epistemic uncertainty.


4     Supervised Finetuning with Self-Distillation
A natural follow-up question is whether the suppression of epistemic verbalization under
high I (y∗ ; c | x ) is merely stylistic or has a tangible impact on reasoning capability. To test
this, we conduct off-policy self-distillation (SFT) using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (Guo
et al., 2025) on two datasets, each containing 800 correct responses:

5     On-Policy Self-Distillation

We now turn to on-policy self-distillation (Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Penaloza
et al., 2026), where the model learns from dense reward signals provided by a self-teacher
with access to the correct solution, based on its own responses. We compare GRPO with
Reinforcement Learning via Self-Distillation (SDPO) (Hübotter et al., 2026) on the DAPO-
Math-17k dataset (Yu et al., 2026), using Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) and DeepSeek-R1-
Distill-Qwen-7B (Guo et al., 2025). Additional results for Qwen3-1.7B and Olmo-3-7B-
Instruct (Olmo et al., 2025) are in Appendix J.3. For each model, we track training score,
response length, and OOD performance on AIME24 and AMC23. We fix the teacher to the
initial policy, which performs better than a moving target (see Section 5.5 for a comparison).
The behavior of on-policy self-distillation depends on two factors: (i) the extent of epistemic
verbalization already present in the base model, and (ii) the richness of the conditioning
context c. To disentangle these, we compare GRPO and SDPO under two settings: c = s
(full solution) and c = s\think (solution without <think> content).

5.1    DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B

DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B is a representative high-reasoning model that generates rich
epistemic verbalizations within <think> tags, averaging about 180 epistemic tokens per
response, as shown in Section 3, leading to lengthy yet high-performing reasoning outputs.

Training Performance         As shown in Figure 3a, GRPO training slightly increases E[ L(y)]
with a modest improvement in score. In contrast, SDPO with c = s causes a sharp initial
drop in both E[ L(y)] and score. Performance gradually recovers but remains below GRPO
throughout training. When the conditioning is reduced to c = s\think , the drop in E[ L(y)] is
attenuated and the score trajectory approaches that of GRPO, consistent with the relationship
between I (y∗ ; c | x ) and epistemic suppression discussed in Section 3.

5.2    Qwen3-8B (Thinking Mode: ON)

Qwen3-8B is a hybrid reasoning model that can operate in either a thinking mode for
deliberate reasoning or a non-thinking mode for direct responses. With thinking mode
enabled, Qwen3-8B produces longer responses than DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, with a
higher number of epistemic tokens (see Appendix E.2 for a comparison of epistemic tokens
across models).

Training Performance         As shown in Figure 4a, E[ L(y)] decreases under both GRPO and
SDPO, with SDPO exhibiting a larger reduction and a correspondingly larger performance
drop. Notably, E[ L(y)] first drops sharply, then increases slightly. Since the teacher policy is
fixed as the reference policy, shortening the response by ∼900 tokens reduces the informa-
tiveness of c, i.e., decreases I (y∗ , c | x ). As the context becomes less informative, the model
compensates by increasing epistemic verbalization, causing the length to partially recover.

OOD Evaluation - AIME24, AMC23             The gap becomes more pronounced on OOD
benchmarks (Figures 4b and 4c): GRPO maintains largely stable performance with grad-
ually decreasing E[ L(y)], whereas SDPO falls below the base model, particularly with
c = s. Notably, although GRPO and SDPO with c = s\think achieve comparable training
performance, their OOD results diverge, especially on the more challenging AIME24, where
SDPO with c = s\think shows progressive performance degradation as training proceeds.

5.5    Ablation Study: Fixed vs. Moving Target Teacher

In naive on-policy self-distillation, the teacher and student share a continuously updated
policy, making the teacher a moving target that can introduce training instability (Zhao
et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). To mitigate this, SDPO uses an EMA-smoothed teacher (EMA
rate: 0.05). However, we find that setting the EMA rate to 0.0 (i.e., fixing the teacher to the
initial policy) yields better performance, and thus Section 5 follows this setting.

6     Relationship Between Task Coverage, Epistemic Verbalization and
      Generalization Ability

Across the off-policy and on-policy settings analyzed above, self-distillation consistently
produces more confident responses with reduced E[ E(y)]. This aligns with the findings of
Hübotter et al. (2026), which reports that SDPO learns to reason concisely: on Science Q&A
(Chemistry, Physics, Biology, and Materials Science) (Feng et al., 2024), tool use (Tang et al.,
2023), and LiveCodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2025), SDPO achieves higher accuracy than GRPO
while producing substantially shorter outputs with fewer epistemic markers.
In other words, in these domains, self-distillation suppresses epistemic verbalization and
improves performance simultaneously. The key question is why the same mechanism leads
to performance degradation in our math-focused setup. We hypothesize that the answer lies in
differences in task coverage between the training and evaluation distributions.

6.1     Comparison of Task Coverage

To test this hypothesis, we compare the dataset characteristics of the settings where SDPO
outperformed GRPO against our experimental setup. As shown in Table 3, the Chemistry
dataset, despite its large size, draws from only six main problem types that differ primarily
in surface details rather than underlying structure. LiveCodeBench v6 contains diverse
problems but only 131 in total, leading to repeated exposure during training with identical
train/eval splits. In contrast, DAPO-Math-17k exposes the model to 14,000 distinct problems
(78% of the 25,600 samples drawn over 100 steps, due to repeated sampling), spanning a
broad, non-overlapping range of problem types, and evaluation is performed on unseen
problem types.

6.2     Relationship Between Task Coverage and Learning Performance

To further investigate the interplay between task coverage and generalization, we vary the
number of training questions |D| ∈ {1, 8, 64, 128, 512} from DAPO-Math-17k and train with
both GRPO and SDPO. All experiments use Qwen3-8B (Thinking Mode OFF).

Training Logs     As shown in Figure 8, GRPO and SDPO exhibit distinct training dynamics
as |D| varies. When |D| ≤ 128, SDPO quickly achieves high scores while reducing E[ L(y)]
by up to 8×, indicating higher training efficiency on a small task set. However, at |D| = 512,
further reductions in E[ L(y)] begin to hurt the training score relative to GRPO, whose
E[ L(y)] gradually increases with |D|.
This difference can be interpreted through task coverage. As |D| grows, the model must
accommodate a broader range of reasoning patterns. GRPO addresses this by increasing
E[ E(y)], allowing the model to express greater uncertainty and adapt its reasoning ac-
cordingly. SDPO instead encourages confident, concise responses—effective when task
coverage is small but limiting when the problem set becomes larger and more diverse.

    The value of epistemic verbalization scales with generalization demands: largely re-

    increasingly important as task diversity grows.



7      Conclusion

In this work, we provide an empirical analysis of on-policy self-distillation, motivated by
an information-theoretic view of conditioning context richness. Our experiments suggest
that the effectiveness of self-distillation is closely tied to how information is provided to the
model and how the model expresses uncertainty during reasoning. We observe that self-
distillation tends to produce answers with higher confidence and shorter reasoning traces.
While this effect enables more compact reasoning and can quickly improve in-domain
performance when task coverage is limited, it becomes less effective when task coverage is
broad and may even harm OOD performance.
Importantly, the fact that performance can degrade even when a mathematically sound
objective function is designed to elicit correct CoT reasoning suggests that the choice
of optimization objective alone may not be sufficient for preserving robust reasoning,
and that we need to pay closer attention to how training reshapes the model’s reasoning
behavior, beyond answer correctness. We hope that our analysis contributes to a deeper
understanding of self-distillation and other LLM post-training methods, and provides
insights for developing more robust training strategies.

research, concerns related to privacy or human subjects ethics do not apply.

ability to appropriately express uncertainty is directly related to model reliability and safety.
Training procedures that indiscriminately suppress such expressions may increase the risk
of models producing incorrect answers with unwarranted confidence. We hope that our
analysis contributes to the development of more robust and trustworthy reasoning models.

A    Related Works

Self-Distillation for LLM Reasoning. Self-distillation, where a single model acts as both
teacher and student under asymmetric conditioning (Snell et al., 2022), has recently been
widely adopted for LLM post-training. SDPO (Hübotter et al., 2026) conditions the self-
teacher on its previously generated correct trajectories or rich environment feedback to
provide dense credit assignment, while OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026) leverages ground-truth
solutions as privileged information, achieving strong efficiency gains in both cases. Our
analysis builds upon the SDPO codebase, and a detailed comparison with OPSD is provided
in Appendix I. Liu et al. (2026); Penaloza et al. (2026); Ye et al. (2026) further utilize this
paradigm in settings that incorporate additional signals, such as privileged information,
self-generated hints derived from environmental interactions, or contextual guidance, with
the goal of internalizing this external knowledge into the model’s parameters.
While these works demonstrate the effectiveness of self-distillation, they do not investigate
when or why self-distillation may degrade reasoning performance, especially in scenarios
where a model must solve problems entirely on its own, without interaction with the
external environment, which is the central question of our work.

Uncertainty Expression in LLM Reasoning. Kim et al. (2026) introduce an information-
theoretic framework that identifies epistemic verbalization, the explicit externalization of
uncertainty, as informationally necessary for robust reasoning. Our work answers a question
this framework cannot readily explain: Under what conditions is this behavior encouraged or
suppressed? And if epistemic verbalization is necessary, why does suppressing it lead to better
performance in some domains, such as Chemistry, while degrading performance in others? We trace
this to two factors: conditioning context richness (Section 3) and task coverage (Section 6),
supported by a systematic analysis of recent on-policy self-distillation methods across
various models with varying pretrained capabilities.

Reasoning Compression. Several recent methods aim to shorten reasoning traces while
preserving accuracy. GFPO (Shrivastava et al., 2026) filters training rollouts by length and
token efficiency within enlarged sample groups, reducing GRPO’s length inflation by 46–85%
on STEM and coding benchmarks. OPSDC (Sang et al., 2026) uses a conciseness instruction
as privileged context, achieving 57–59% token reduction on MATH-500. ConPress (Deng
et al., 2026) exploits multi-question contextual pressure to elicit self-compressed traces,
yielding 30–60% reductions via SFT. Accordion-Thinking (Yang et al., 2026) trains models to
produce periodic step summaries that can be discarded, achieving 3× throughput with a
gap-vanishing phenomenon during RL training. CEEH (Luo et al., 2026) proposes difficulty-
aware entropy regularization to prevent entropy collapse under length constraints.
Our analysis complements these efforts by showing that compression which suppresses
uncertainty expression, even when producing correct answers, can harm OOD generaliza-
tion, and that this effect is modulated by task coverage. Therefore, the key challenge is to
filter out non-informative content while retaining epistemic expressions that enable iterative
belief refinement, rather than blindly compressing the reasoning process.

B        Basis for Selecting Epistemic Tokens
To provide an empirical basis for our selection of epistemic tokens, we analyzed uncertainty
expressions across a diverse set of reasoning models. We collected 9,600 reasoning traces
from eight models spanning four model families, including the Qwen3 family (Yang et al.,
2025) (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-32B), the QwQ family (Yang et al.,
2025) (QwQ-32B), the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen family (Guo et al., 2025) (7B and 32B), and
the OLMo family (Olmo et al., 2025) (OLMo-3-7B-Instruct).
The traces were generated on 600 math problems drawn from AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023,
and MATH500, with two samples per problem for each model. Each reasoning trace was
then provided to GPT-5 with the following prompt:
     Prompt for Epistemic Token Extraction

     From the reasoning trace below, extract every span where the model expresses uncertainty or
     doubt (hesitation, second-guessing, hedging, suspicion something is wrong).
     For each span return:
     1. ‘span’: the exact uncertainty sentence(s), copied verbatim;
     2. ‘trigger token’: the single short token or phrase (1–3 words, lowercased) inside that span
        that signals the uncertainty.

This procedure yielded over 99,000 (span, trigger token) pairs. We then ranked trigger tokens
by frequency within each model family (Qwen3, QwQ, DeepSeek, and OLMo).
As shown in Table 4, the ten markers maybe, wait, check, hmm, seems, perhaps, might, actually,
likely, and alternatively, together with their natural variants, consistently appeared among
the top-ranked uncertainty expressions, supporting their inclusion across model families.
While several tokens, such as let me try/verify, I think, and make sure, were excluded, our
selected markers cover 75–93% of the occurrences of the phrases in Table 4.

C     Experimental Details

Training     For GRPO and SDPO training, we built upon the SDPO implementation (https:
//github.com/lasgroup/SDPO) and additionally incorporated the DAPO-Math-17k dataset.
The original DAPO-Math-17k dataset uses the following prompt format:

           Solve the following math problem step by step. The last line of your
           response should be of the form Answer: $Answer (without quotes)
           where $Answer is the answer to the problem.\n\n{question}\nRemember
           to put your answer on its own line after "Answer:".

We replaced this with a simpler format:

           {question}\nPlease reason step by step, and put your final answer
           within \boxed{}.

as we observed that this format consistently yielded higher evaluation performance. For
reward verification, we used the scoring function from the verl framework, which extracts
the answer from the \boxed{} expression and verifies correctness via exact match followed
by mathematical equivalence checking using math-verify, adapted from EleutherAI’s lm-
evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2024).

experiments on the relationship between task coverage and learning performance in Figure
9, we reduced the question batch size to 64 due to the use of smaller training questions.

responses. For each word w ∈ V and model m ∈ {base, GRPO, SDPO}, we compute the
average per-response frequency:

I               Comparison with OPSD from Zhao et al. (2026)

Recently, Zhao et al. (2026) proposed OPSD and demonstrated performance gains in math-
ematical reasoning through on-policy self-distillation, particularly on the Qwen3 series.
Unlike our setup, where both the student and the teacher either enable or disable thinking
mode, OPSD adopts a hybrid configuration in which the student operates with thinking
mode disabled while the teacher has it enabled.
As our experiments also confirm, enabling thinking mode produces substantially longer
responses with a greater number of epistemic tokens, making this hybrid setup function
more akin to conventional teacher–student distillation, despite using the same underlying
model. We note that this configuration is inherently limited to model families such as
Qwen3 that support toggling thinking mode on and off.
For training efficiency, OPSD trains only on a response prefix (1,024 tokens by default),
rather than the full student response. Unlike OPSD, SDPO and our setup use full fine-tuning
with verl (Sheng et al., 2024), whereas OPSD adopts LoRA fine-tuning with trl (von Werra
et al., 2020) and a smaller batch size of 32 (vs. 256 in our setup), resulting in higher training
efficiency but smaller parameter updates per step.

of training. However, as training progresses, performance steadily deteriorates despite this
early improvement. In contrast, under our homogeneous setup, where both the student and
teacher operate with thinking mode enabled (pink line), performance declines consistently
throughout training. Response length and epistemic token count likewise decrease steadily,
consistent with our analysis.
While this hybrid distillation setup for the Qwen3 series presents an interesting research
direction with its own unique training dynamics, such as why performance initially im-
proves before declining and whether this stems from changes in reasoning behavior or chat
template mismatch, a thorough investigation is beyond the scope of this work and is left for
future exploration.

J.2     Qwen3-1.7B (Thinking Mode: ON)

To investigate whether our findings generalize to smaller models, we conducted the same on-
policy self-distillation experiment on Qwen3-1.7B. As shown in Figure 21, our finding that
self-distillation reduces uncertainty and makes outputs more concise, while simultaneously
degrading mathematical reasoning performance, remains consistent across model sizes.

to Qwen3-8B with Thinking Mode ON (12.1%) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (40.7%).
This suggests that the externalization of uncertainty may be particularly important for
robust reasoning in smaller models, which possess more limited inherent capabilities.
