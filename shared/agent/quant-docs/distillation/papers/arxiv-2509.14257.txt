The Use of Large Language Models (LLMs) in Writing
An LLM (i.e., OpenAI’s GPT-5) was used solely for minor language editing, including grammar correction and slight
rephrasing for clarity. It did not contribute to the research design, and all scientific content is entirely the authors’ own.

A. Relation to Closely Related Work
Why traditional DAgger cannot be directly used in agent distillation: DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) was designed in 2011
for classical imitation-learning domains (e.g., robotic control), where (i) the state is well-defined and can be judged locally,
and (ii) expert labels are treated as reliable supervision. These assumptions break down for LLM agents that act through
long-horizon, partially observable, tool-augmented interaction.
Our SCoRe method evaluates the entire trajectory and identifies mistakes only after seeing the global trajectory, which is
necessary to correctly pinpoint where errors occurred. In contrast, DAgger forces the teacher to correct the agent at every
step, even though many actions reveal themselves as right or wrong only later through tool feedback or final success or
failure. This is suitable for traditional imitation learning but not for agent distillation. Moreover, the teacher model itself can
make mistakes or contain noise; DAgger fully trusts the teacher, whereas SCoRe automatically filters out teacher errors,
which is crucial.
Other Closely Related Work: SCoRe is related to prior correction-based methods such as LEMMA (Pan et al., 2025),
which identify an erroneous step and regenerate from that point. Its key distinction is that SCoRe remains student-centered,
which is also the main focus of this paper: after correction, the student itself continues the trajectory, and the resulting
verified prefix is further used for short-horizon RL. This strict student-centered design is required by our theory.
SCoRe is also related to PRM-style methods with step-level rewards (Zhang et al., 2025), but it does not train a separate
process reward model. Instead, it uses first-error localization on student-generated trajectories to define both rollout starting
points and localized rewards.

unconditional guarantee for all practical LLM-agent settings.

Supervised Fine-Tuning. For BC initialization and SCoRe-SFT, we fine-tune all backbones using the LLaMAFactory
framework (Zheng et al., 2024) with a learning rate of 7 × 10−6 , the AdamW optimizer with weight decay 0.1, and a global

for efficiency, with BF16 mixed precision and a maximum sequence length of 4096 tokens. All tool execution outputs are

that the model focuses on reasoning quality rather than memorizing tool responses. The training data consists of two distinct
categories: search and math. For BC initialization, we use 2,031 search trajectories and 2,080 math trajectories, while
SCoRe-SFT employs 4,990 search and 5,019 math trajectories for correction-based fine-tuning.
Reinforcement Learning. The SCoRe-RL phase performs short-horizon fine-tuning using the GRPO algorithm (Shao et al.,
2024) within the VERL framework (Sheng et al., 2025). We adopt a global batch size of 128, a PPO mini-batch size of 16, a
rollout size of 16, and a maximum response length of 4,096 tokens. Training is conducted for 3 epochs on 8×NVIDIA H20
GPUs, using 5,271 search trajectories and 5,639 math trajectories.
For factual reasoning datasets, the search tool is implemented via the Google Search API. To reduce tool-call latency
during both training and inference, we include only the textual snippets returned by the API as the [Observation]
content, omitting full browser navigation or long-form summarization. This design substantially reduces retrieval time while
preserving sufficient context to answer queries effectively.

D. Additional Experiment Results
More baselines in reasoning benchmarks. Table 7 compares CoT Distillation, Agent Distillation, and our proposed
SCoRe-RL, with all methods evaluated using the exact-match metric on math reasoning benchmarks, following (Kang
et al., 2025). CoT Distillation trains smaller models to replicate step-by-step reasoning traces generated by large LLMs via
Chain-of-Thought prompting. While simple and effective, its reliance on static reasoning limits generalization to novel
knowledge or precise computation and can lead to hallucinations. Agent Distillation (Kang et al., 2025) transfers both
reasoning and tool-use skills from LLM agents by imitating complete [Thought-Action-Observation] trajectories.
Its training process is similar to our BC baseline in the main experiments (Tables 1 and 2), but introduces additional
enhancements: a first-thought prefix to produce higher-quality trajectories and a self-consistent action generation strategy to
improve inference robustness.
Agent Distillation has been shown to outperform vanilla CoT Distillation by incorporating structured, agent-style reasoning
into the distillation process. SCoRe-RL further exploits correction-based trajectories and RL to deliver substantial per-
formance gains. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, SCoRe-RL attains an average score of 49.3, a +7.5 improvement over
Agent Distillation (41.8), with notable gains on challenging benchmarks such as AIME (+5.6) and OlymMATH (+4.6).
On Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, it raises the average score to 42.1, +5.0 over Agent Distillation (37.1). These results
demonstrate that SCoRe-RL enables small models to achieve significant improvements beyond existing distillation baselines,

More baselines in deep search benchmarks. We additionally implement three stronger baselines, and compare with them
on the deep search benchmarks.
First, we implement a PRM-style baseline (BC + GRPO (Step Reward)), where the teacher evaluates each Thought–Action–
Observation step following the general setup of Zhang et al. (2025). The reward is defined as the proportion of steps judged
to move toward the correct solution, providing denser teacher-guided supervision than binary task-success rewards.
Besides, we implement On-Policy Distillation (OPD) (Lu & Lab, 2025). Since OPD typically requires the teacher and
student to come from the same model family, for the deep search setting with a Qwen3-8B student we use the largest dense
Qwen3 model as the teacher. This teacher is slightly stronger than the main teacher used in SCoRe.
We also implement ReLIFT (Ma et al., 2026), an interleaved RL–SFT baseline originally proposed for reasoning tasks.
Since our setting involves agentic reasoning with tool use, the number of hardest samples, i.e., queries on which all rollout
attempts fail, is relatively small. To provide ReLIFT with sufficient online SFT data, we use twice the training data, with
seed question–answer pairs drawn from ToolStar, the same source as ours.
The results in Table 8 suggest that teacher guidance alone is not sufficient. The PRM-style baseline already provides
dense teacher supervision at every step, yet it still underperforms SCoRe-RL. As illustrated in Figure 1, SCoRe retains
teacher corrections only when they are validated by the student’s success, and further combines such verified guidance with
short-horizon RL. We believe this coupling of verification and localized policy optimization is a key advantage of SCoRe.
On-policy distillation also underperforms SCoRe. A likely reason is that it directly imitates teacher trajectories that may not
be well matched to the student’s current capabilities. Although this mismatch may be reduced with substantially more data
and compute as the student improves, under our training budget SCoRe remains more effective.
SCoRe-RL also outperforms ReLIFT. We suppose that short-horizon RL provides a more stable optimization signal in our
setting, whereas ReLIFT may require a larger training budget and more ReLIFT-style hard samples to achieve comparable
performance.
